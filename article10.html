從秦制到大運河：中國帝國如何用基礎設施對抗地理與分裂

從標準統一到物動員——基礎設施視角下的帝國興衰邏輯

引言

一部中國帝國史，其底層是一部基礎設施史。秦始皇在公元前二二一年統一度量衡，漢承秦制而損益之，然而公元二二〇年漢亡之後近四百年分裂，將這一切徹底摧毀。隋文帝於五八九年重新統一中國，不得不從零重建度量衡與貨幣；而支撐這一重建的，是一套史無前例的運河網絡。運河耗盡了隋朝的民力，卻為唐、金、元、明、清諸代留下數百年的基礎設施紅利，並最終把北京從邊疆軍鎮推上帝國首都的位置。本文沿這條因果鏈展開，考察中國帝國如何以運河與道路對抗地理約束與政治分裂，並以朝鮮三國與高句麗的故事，作為這套邏輯在東亞國際體系中的鏡像。

第一章 秦的統一標準及其脆弱性

公元前二二一年，秦始皇（Qin Shi Huang）完成中國歷史上第一次大一統，隨即推行「書同文、車同軌、統一度量衡」。這不是簡單的行政措施，而是一場深刻的經濟革命。在此之前，各諸侯國的尺、斗、斤、幣各不相同，跨區域貿易必須反覆換算；統一之後，秦以中央強制力為後盾，鑄造標準銅權、銅量，發行「半兩錢」（Ban Liang coin），使一個橫跨黃河與長江的龐大經濟體，第一次擁有了統一的計量語言。《史記·秦始皇本紀》載其「一法度衡石丈尺，車同軌，書同文字」（譯文：統法令與度量衡標準，車輪軌距相同，書寫文字統一），寥寥數語，道盡一場靜默的革命。

然而，這套標準的命運與秦朝的命運緊緊綁在一起。秦始皇死於公元前二一〇年，秦朝在前二〇七年崩潰，立國僅十五年。秦的標準依賴中央政權的強制執行力：郡縣官吏依中央頒發的「母本」校核地方度量衡，私鑄與私制度量衡者受嚴刑。一旦中央權威瓦解，執行鏈條便隨之斷裂。秦末戰火中，官方標準器被熔毀或散失，地方重新回到各自為政的狀態。

漢朝（Han Dynasty，前二〇二—二二〇年）建立後，出現了中國制度史上一個關鍵現象——「漢承秦制」（Han cheng Qin zhi）。漢高祖劉邦並未另起爐灶，而是全盤繼承秦的官僚體系、法律框架與度量衡標準。漢初繼續使用秦的「半兩錢」，至漢武帝（Han Wudi）時期進行重大幣制改革，廢除半兩錢，發行「五銖錢」（Wu Zhu coin）。五銖錢形制規整、重量標準，此後流通約七百年，成為中國歷史上壽命最長的鑄幣。漢武帝又統一鹽鐵官營、推行均輸平準，把帝國的經濟控制力推向高峰。

因此，在兩漢四百餘年間，中國確實擁有過一套行之有效的統一標準。問題不在於漢朝沒有標準，而在於漢朝的標準同樣綁定於漢朝的中央政權。公元二二〇年漢亡，這套標準便失去了保障者。

第二章 四百年分裂與標準的毀滅

漢亡之後，中國進入歷史上最漫長的分裂期。先是三國（Three Kingdoms，二二〇—二八〇年）鼎立，魏、蜀、吳各自鑄錢、各自徵稅。西晉（Western Jin，二六五—三一六年）於二八〇年滅吳，短暫統一，然統一僅維持三十餘年。西晉皇室內部爆發「八王之亂」（War of the Eight Princes，二九一—三〇六年），國力耗盡；北方諸族起兵，三一六年洛陽陷落，西晉滅亡。晉室南渡，建立東晉（Eastern Jin，三一七—四二〇年），只控制長江以南；北方則進入「十六國」（Sixteen Kingdoms）時期，政權更迭如走馬燈。東晉之後，南方相繼為宋、齊、梁、陳四朝，北方由北魏（Northern Wei）統一，後又分裂為東魏、西魏、北齊、北周。史稱南北朝（Southern and Northern Dynasties，四二〇—五八九年）。

這近四百年裡，南北經濟各自演化，度量衡與貨幣全面分化。北魏的一「斤」約為南朝的兩倍，南北的「尺」長度不同，「斗」容量不同。貨幣方面，各國頻繁鑄幣，成色與重量參差不齊；私鑄盛行，劣幣驅逐良幣，民間逐漸退回到以絹帛、糧食為媒介的物物交換。北朝曾一度廢錢用帛，南朝則屢次鑄行鐵錢、鉛錢，幣值劇烈波動。《隋書·食貨志》記當時「錢貨雜用，姦偽競生」（譯文：錢幣與實物混用，欺詐偽劣層出不窮），正是這一混亂的寫照。

這裡必須強調一個邏輯：秦與漢的標準不是設計失敗，而是保障它的國家不存在了。 標準是一種公共品，其供給依賴中央權威的強制與校核。分裂意味著多個權威並存，每個權威都按自身財政需要調整標準；數百年下來，標準不僅失去統一，甚至失去了「母本」——沒有任何一方擁有可以回溯的原始標準器。當隋文帝在五八九年重新統一中國時，他面對的不是一套「被遺忘的舊標準」，而是一片「標準已經不存在」的經濟廢墟。

第三章 隋文帝：從零重建標準與大運河網絡

隋文帝（Sui Wendi，楊堅）於五八一年受禪建隋，五八九年滅陳，結束近四百年分裂。他面對的首要經濟問題，正是度量衡與貨幣的全面混亂。

隋文帝的應對是系統性的。第一，統一度量衡：頒布標準尺、斗、秤，要求各地依式製造並定期校核。第二，整頓貨幣：鑄造新的標準「五銖錢」，嚴禁私鑄，在關口設卡沒收非標準錢幣，使貨幣重新成為可信的交易媒介。第三，整頓戶籍與賦稅：推行「大索貌閱」與「輸籍定樣」，清查隱漏人口，重建國家財政基礎。

本文開篇所引隋文帝詔書——「自晉室南遷，天下喪亂，四海分裂……車軌不同，民陷塗炭」——正是他對這一歷史廢墟的自述。詔書所言「車軌不同」，即是度量衡與制度的分裂；「上天降鑒，命朕主之」，則是他以重建統一標準為己任的政治宣示。《隋書·高祖紀》亦載其「統一寰宇，百度惟新」（譯文：統一夭下，各項制度煥然一新），可與此詔相印證。

重建標準之外，隋文帝的另一大貢獻是啟動了大運河網絡。他先於五八四年開鑿廣通渠（Guangtong Canal），自首都長安（Chang'an）附近的渭水通往黃河，繞開淺沙多灘、無法通行大船的渭水河段。修渠的動因，是首都的糧食問題：長安位於關中平原（Guanzhong Plain），四周有山脈與關隘環繞，易守難攻，但本地農業在千餘年高強度耕作後地力衰退，無法養活統一帝國龐大的官僚、軍隊與城市人口。糧食必須從華北平原——今河南、河北、山東一帶——運來；而黃河轉渭水的水路在潼關（Tongguan）附近極為難行，糧船常被迫卸貨，改由牛車陸運數百里，成本高昂、損耗巨大。廣通渠的修建，使糧船可以繞過渭水淺灘，直抵長安，是統一帝國供養首都的「最後一公里」。他後來又疏浚山陽瀆，連接淮河與長江，為南北貫通奠定基礎。運河網絡，由此發端。

第四章 大運河網絡：規模、目的與民力代價

廣通渠只是開始。隋文帝之子隋煬帝（Emperor Yang，楊廣）將運河擴展為連接五大水系的全國網絡，總長逾兩千七百公里。

  • 通濟渠（Tongji Canal，六〇五年）：自東都洛陽（Luoyang）東南行，接入淮河，再通長江，把華北平原的糧食與物資直接輸往洛陽。
  • 山陽瀆（Shanyang Canal）：隋文帝五八七年疏浚，隋煬帝約六〇五年擴建，連接淮河與長江，是南北貫通的關鍵一段。
  • 永濟渠（Yongji Canal，六〇八年）：自黃河向北，直抵涿郡（Zhuojun，今北京），專為征伐高句麗（Goguryeo）供應軍需而建。
  • 江南河（Jiangnan Canal，六一〇年）：自長江向南至杭州（Hangzhou），汲取長江三角洲的財富。

至六一〇年，隋朝建成了連接海河、黃河、淮河、長江、錢塘江五大水系的南北大動脈。這套網絡的設計目的有三：其一，把南方的糧食與財富輸往北方首都與邊防；其二，為對外戰爭提供後勤；其三，把新統一的國家在經濟上縫合起來，使南北不再是兩個被征服的半壁，而是一個可以內部循環的整體。這與隋文帝統一度量衡、貨幣的邏輯完全一致——他統一的不只是計量單位，還有貨物的物理流動。

然而，運河的代價是慘烈的，也是隋朝亡國的重要因素之一。第一，民力徵發。 隋煬帝徵發數百萬農民，史載通濟渠一段即徵發民夫逾百萬，部分河段半數勞工死於勞累、疾病與虐待，屍體被埋入新挖的溝渠。《隋書·食貨志》記當時「役丁死者什四五」（譯文：服役的壯丁死了十分之四五），數字或有過甚，然民力之竭可見一斑。第二，農業崩潰。 青壯勞力被抽離農田數年，播種與收割無人，糧食產量驟降，饑荒蔓延。第三，賦稅壓榨。 運河、宮殿、長城與三次遠征高句麗的費用疊加，賦稅與徭役超出農民承受極限，逃亡與抗稅遍及全國。第四，民怨與象徵性羞辱。 隋煬帝沿運河進行奢華的龍舟巡遊，隨行船隊綿延千里，沿途州縣被迫供應，民間視運河為皇帝虛榮的工具，而非公共福祉。

於是，運河工程、苛稅與遠征高句麗三者疊加，壓垮了隋朝。六一八年，隋煬帝在揚州（Yangzhou）被部下所殺，隋朝立國三十七年而亡。歷史的諷刺在於，後繼的唐朝（Tang Dynasty，六一八—九〇七年）坐享其成——「隋朝開河，唐朝受益」（Sui built the canal, Tang reaped the benefits），唐朝的繁榮在相當程度上建立於隋朝以民力換來的基礎設施之上。

第五章 道路系統：帝國的神經系統

如果說運河是帝國的動脈，道路就是神經系統。運河解決「量大」的問題——糧食無法靠車運規模化；道路解決「快」的問題——軍隊與情報必須以天、以小時計。

秦修秦直道（Qinzhi Dao）：從首都區域直通北方邊境，長約七百至八百公里，穿山填谷，路面寬可多車並行，被稱為「世界第一條高速公路」。它幾乎完全出於軍事目的：匈奴來襲時，秦軍可沿直道迅速北上，無需繞行山谷。

漢鑿河西走廊（Hexi Corridor）路線：以哨所（outpost）、駐軍（garrison）、烽火台（beacon tower）連成鏈條，把帝國力量投射到中亞，設立西域都護府，首次將今新疆納入中國勢力範圍。

唐完善驛站（Yizhan）體系：四萬餘公里道路、一千六百餘驛站、換馬接力，緊急軍情可達每日二百五十公里。這套系統的效率，使唐廷能夠對邊疆危機作出快速反應。《唐六典》載「凡三十里一驛，天下凡一千六百三十有九所」（譯文：每三十里設一驛站，全國共有一千六百三十九所），可見其制度之精密。

明修九邊（Nine Frontier Garrisons）軍道：九個軍事防區自西向東橫貫北疆，軍道連接各鎮，專為輸送火炮、糧草與兵員而建。

水陸並行的邏輯至此完整：運河運糧，道路運兵；運河慢而量巨，道路快而量小；兩者互補，構成帝國的物流骨架。

第六章 北京：從邊疆軍鎮到帝國首都

隋之前，涿郡/北京從未做過統一王朝的首都。它一直是邊疆要塞：周代薊國（Ji state）、戰國燕國（Yan state）、秦的郡（commandery）、唐的幽州（Youzhou）軍鎮。它是對抗北方遊牧勢力的前哨，而非帝國的中心。這一身份持續了上千年——北京長期是「守邊之城」，不是「治天下之城」。

轉折點來自隋朝。隋朝把永濟渠（Yongji Canal）的北端終點設在涿郡，將其建為軍事補給站（depot，軍需庫）。這一決定證明了一個革命性的事實：憑藉運河運糧，北京可以養活龐大的人口與軍隊。遠征高句麗進一步強化了北京的軍事定位，也對其後勤能力做了一次壓力測試：雖然戰爭失敗，但北京作為北方軍事樞紐的地位被固定下來。隋朝的投資，為北京日後的首都地位埋下了第一塊基石。

金朝（Jin Dynasty，一一一五—一二三四年）於一一五三年定都中都（Zhongdu，今北京），其鼎盛疆域約二百三十萬至二百五十萬平方公里，橫跨華北、滿洲、蒙古東部及俄羅斯遠東部分地區，統治百餘年，證明北京可以作為首都良好運轉。金人修金口河（Jin Kou Canal）、閘河（Zha River Canal），接通運河網絡，使漕糧可以持續入城。元朝（Yuan Dynasty，一二七一—一三六八年）在相同位置建大都（Dadu/Khanbaliq），並截直運河，開鑿會通河、通惠河；清朝（Qing）延續。

然而，金、元、清三代皆是少數民族王朝，其龍興之地在北方或東北，定都北京有其「雙面首都」的內在邏輯——南面治漢地，北顧聯本族。真正值得追問的是：一個漢族王朝，為何也會放棄自己的龍興之地，把首都從南方遷到北方？ 這就是明朝成祖朱棣從南京遷都北京的故事。它不是簡單的「繼承前朝舊制」，而是一場深思熟慮、代價巨大、且深刻改變了此後五百年中國政治格局的戰略抉擇。

一、南京：明朝的起點

明朝開國皇帝朱元璋（明太祖）於二三六八年稱帝，定都應天（Yingtian，今南京）。南京作為首都，有其充分的理由：它是朱元璋起家的根據地，是其政權合法性的源頭；它地處江南，靠近全國最富庶的糧倉，漕運便利、財賦充盈；它還有長江天險，易守難攻。洪武一朝三十一年，南京作為首都運轉良好，宮城、官署、城牆相繼建成，制度漸備。

但南京作為統一帝國的首都，有一個先天的短板：它太靠南了。 明朝建立後，最大的軍事威脅仍是退回草原的蒙古勢力（北元及其後的韃靼、瓦剌）。南京距北方邊境數千里，邊關急報傳到御前需十餘日，皇帝對北方戰事的反應永遠慢一拍。朱元璋晚年已意識到這一問題，曾考慮遷都，派太子朱標巡視關中與洛陽，考察遷都可行性；然朱標早逝，朱元璋亦年邁，遷都之議遂寢。

二、朱棣為什麼遷都北京？

靖難之役後，燕王朱棣（明成祖）於一四〇二年奪位稱帝，仍以南京為都。但遷都之念，自他登基之日起便已萌生。其動因，可歸為四層：

第一，北京是他的龍興之地與權力根基。 朱棣以燕王身份鎮守北平（Beijing）二十餘年，其軍事班底、親信將領、地方勢力皆在北方。南京是建文帝舊臣的天下，他在南京始終有「客居」之感。遷都北京，等於把自己的根基搬到權力中心，一勞永逸地解決合法性焦慮。

第二，軍事防禦的現實需要。 明朝最大的威脅在北方草原。朱棣本人是馬上皇帝，五次親征蒙古，深知「天子守國門」的道理。他曾在詔書中直言：「北平，朕之舊封，有宮室王府，且形勢足以控馭朔漠。」（譯文：北平是朕的舊日封地，有現成的宮室王府，而且地勢足以控制北方大漠。）把首都設在前線，既是對邊將的威懾，也是對軍心的凝聚。

第三，經濟與漕運的條件已經成熟。 朱棣遷都北京，並非不顧糧食問題。他深知北京無法自給，遂大力疏浚大運河——永樂九年（一四一一年）命宋禮、陳瑄等重開會通河、疏浚通惠河，使江南漕糧可以直抵北京。運河的暢通，是遷都得以成立的前提。 這一點，與隋朝以運河供養北方首都是同一條邏輯的延續：沒有運河，北京養不活首都；有了運河，北京才可能成為首都。

第四，政治象徵意義。 朱棣以「靖難」為名奪位，需要一場宏大的政治工程來重塑合法性。營建北京、遷都北方，既是「天子守國門」的宣示，也是「繼承前朝舊都、統御華夷」的象徵。永樂四年（一四〇六年）起，北京宮城（即後來的紫禁城）開始營建；永樂十八年（一四二〇年）建成，次年正式遷都。

三、遷都的代價與爭議

遷都北京並非沒有反對聲音。永樂年間，朝中大臣多有異議，認為南京財賦充盈、漕運便利，北京僻處北陲、供給艱難。朱棣以強硬手段壓制異議，但遷都的實際成本確實巨大：營建紫禁城與北京城耗費無數人力物力；漕運制度必須全面重建，每年數百萬石漕糧自江南北運，成為明朝財政的沉重負擔；北京周邊地力有限，一旦運河受阻，首都便有斷糧之虞。

然而，從長時段看，遷都北京的收益遠大於成本。明成祖之後的二百餘年，北京作為首都，有效整合了北方的軍事防禦與南方的經濟供給：皇帝坐鎮北方，直接掌控邊防；運河把南方的漕糧源源北運，供養首都與邊軍。「以北方之兵，制南方之財」，這條格局在明朝最終定型，並被清朝全盤繼承。

四、一條完整的歷史線索

至此，一條自隋至清的首都遷移線索可以完整串聯：

  • 隋：修永濟渠至涿郡，把北京建為軍事補給站，證明運河可以供養北方。
  • 金：定都中都，修金口河、閘河，首次以北京為首都，證明北京可以運轉。
  • 元：建大都，截直運河，把北京確立為統一帝國的首都。
  • 明：成祖以「天子守國門」為由，從南京遷都北京，把漢族王朝的首都也定在這裡，並以重開運河為其前提。
  • 清：繼承明的首都格局，繼續以運河供養北京。

這條線索的每一步，都依賴同一個前提：運河必須暢通。 隋朝挖的「管道」，金朝接通了「水龍頭」，元朝把管道拉直，明朝重開淤塞的河段並以遷都為其最終確認，清朝持續維護。北京之所以能成為五百年的帝國首都，不是因為它在任何單項上最優，而是因為它把「軍事防禦的最高分」與「運河物流的可維持性」結合在了一起。

第七章 為什麼定都北方？

在討論北京之前，須先看清一個更大的格局：隋唐以前，統一王朝的首都幾乎都在北方。秦都咸陽（Xianyang），西漢都長安，東漢都洛陽（Luoyang），西晉仍都洛陽，隋唐兩代東西兩京並立，仍以長安、洛陽為主。北宋都開封（Kaifeng），已略偏東南，但仍在黃河一線。真正把首都穩定在更北的北京，是金、元、明、清四代的事。

這一格局並非偶然。若單以經濟論，南方遠勝北方：長江下游自唐以後便是全國糧倉與財賦重心，「蘇湖熟，天下足」之諺即出於此。若單以交通論，南方水網密布，漕運便利，杭州、揚州皆是天下通津。然而歷代王朝寧願以運河把南方的糧食千里北運，也不肯把首都南遷。為什麼？因為首都的選擇，從來不是經濟問題，而是生存問題。帝國的優先級排序中，軍事防禦與政治合法性高於經濟便利；經濟上的不便，可以用基礎設施彌補；防禦上的空缺，卻可能在一夜之間斷送王朝。

一、北方草原是最大威脅

這是最根本、也最不容妥協的一條。自秦始皇修築長城以來，中國帝國的主要軍事威脅始終來自北方草原：秦漢之際的匈奴（Xiongnu）、隋唐之際的突厥（Turks）、宋明之際的蒙古（Mongols）與女真（Jurchens），無一不是從北方壓來。遊牧騎兵機動性極強，一晝夜可馳數百里；若首都在南方，等邊關急報傳到御前，敵軍早已突破長城、深入腹地。

因此，歷代皇帝把首都設成一座前進基地（forward operating base）：皇帝離軍隊越近，決策鏈越短，反應越快。秦都咸陽、漢都長安，皆在關中，居高臨下俯瞰北方；明成祖遷都北京，更直接以「天子守國門」為理由——皇帝親自坐鎮國門，既是對邊將的威懾，也是對軍心的凝聚。反觀南宋，退守杭州（Lin'an），遠離前線，雖有長江天險，卻始終只能被動防守，最終被蒙古自北而南席捲。首都在哪裡，戰略重心就在哪裡；戰略重心在哪裡，帝國的生死線就在哪裡。

二、政治合法性：天命所繫，不可輕棄

第二條是政治與文化上的。中國文明的傳統核心在黃河流域——關中與中原，是周、秦、漢、唐的龍興之地，是禮樂制度、典章文物、世家大族匯聚之所。從歷史中心統治，才符合「天命」（Mandate of Heaven）的敘事：皇帝在長安、洛陽祭天祀祖，接續的是三代以來的正統；若南遷，則等於自認偏安，失去「天下共主」的資格。

這一點在歷史上反覆應驗。一一二七年靖康之變，宋室南渡，定都杭州。南宋雖經濟繁榮、文化鼎盛，卻始終被史家視為「偏安一隅」的殘存政權；北伐之議，終南宋一朝從未停歇，正因為「恢復中原」才是其合法性的來源。再往前看，東晉（Eastern Jin）南渡之後，北方士族與南方土著之間的「僑舊之爭」持續百年，其根源亦在於：南渡政權始終不敢承認自己已經放棄了北方，因為一旦承認，天命便不再屬於自己。

三、首都選擇的公式

把上述因素抽象化，可以寫成一條粗略的公式：

首都 =（軍事防禦 + 政治合法性 + 威脅管理）−（物流成本 + 地方反對）

代入歷代首都，結果一目了然：

  • 杭州、揚州：物流成本極低（位於糧倉與運河樞紐），地方反對亦少；但軍事防禦近乎零分（長江一旦失守便無險可守），統一帝國的政治合法性亦不足。故其宜為經濟都會，不宜為帝國首都。
  • 長安、洛陽：軍事防禦與政治合法性俱高，但物流成本極高（關中地力衰退，必須依賴漕運），且關中地方勢力盤根錯節。故隋唐兩代必須傾全國之力修運河、疏漕路，以補物流之短。
  • 北京：軍事防禦最高（直面草原），威脅管理最直接（皇帝親臨前線），政治合法性在元明清三代由少數民族王朝賦予新的內涵；物流成本同樣極高，靠隋唐以來持續擴建的運河網絡彌補。

皇帝們算的正是這筆賬：寧願承受「物流稅」，也不願承受「無防禦」的生存風險。 運河可以修，糧食可以運，成本再高也是可計算的；而首都一旦失守，王朝即刻傾覆，這是不可計算的。

四、控制貴族與地方勢力

還有一條常被忽略、卻極為現實的因素：首都的位置，也是皇帝與地方勢力博弈的產物。

南方自唐以後日益富庶，江南士族、商幫、鹽商積累了巨大的經濟實力。若首都在南方，皇帝便身處這些地方勢力的包圍之中，容易被挾持、被架空。把首都設在北方，朝廷與南方的富庶保持一定距離，既便於以漕運汲取南方財富，又便於以北方軍事集團制衡南方經濟集團。元、明、清三代定都北京，同時以運河把南方的漕糧源源北運，正是這種「以北方之兵，制南方之財」格局的體現。

反過來，南方的財富也並非全無話語權。南宋定都杭州，某種程度上就是南方經濟集團與南渡朝廷妥協的結果：朝廷需要南方的錢糧，南方需要朝廷的保護，雙方在杭州達成平衡。但這種平衡是脆弱的——一旦北方強敵壓境，缺乏戰略縱深的南方首都便首當其衝。

五、小結：北京是這條邏輯的終點

把以上四條合起來看，北京之所以最終成為帝國首都，並非因為它在任何單項上最優，而是因為它在軍事防禦與威脅管理這兩項上得分最高，而物流上的短板，恰好可以由隋唐以來持續建設的運河網絡來彌補。

金、元、清三代是少數民族王朝，其龍興之地皆在北方或東北，定都北京既可南面而治漢地，又可北顧而聯本族，是「雙面首都」的典型；明朝成祖以燕王起家，北京是其根基，遷都北京既是政治需要，也是軍事需要。於是，一條自秦咸陽、漢長安、隋唐長安洛陽、北宋開封一路東移北移的首都軌跡，最終在元明清三代定格於北京。

這一定格不是終點，而是一個長期博弈的暫時平衡：南方的糧食與財富，北方的軍隊與邊防，運河是連接兩者的臍帶，而皇帝坐在臍帶的北端，一手握著刀，一手握著漕糧。首都在哪裡，從來不是地理問題，而是帝國如何分配其生存資源的問題。

第八章 薩爾水之戰通向朝鮮三國史

薩爾水之戰成為隋亡的重要原因之一。隋煬帝三征高句麗，動用的民力與財力超出帝國承受極限：運河工程、軍需轉運、數百萬征夫，三者疊加，使「耕稼失時，田疇多荒」（《隋書·食貨志》，譯文：農時耽誤，田地荒蕪）。而戰勝者一方，高句麗，也因連年抗隋而元氣大損：戰爭耗盡了它的國庫、人力與農業，山城防禦雖勝，卻無力恢復。這正是慘勝（Pyrrhic victory）的含義——贏下戰役，卻輸掉元氣。

隋亡之後，唐朝接過了這個未完成的課題。唐太宗（Emperor Taizong，李世民）於六四五年親征高句麗，雖未攻克安市城（Ansi City），卻進一步消耗了高句麗。高句麗內亂隨之而來：淵蓋蘇文（Yeon Gaesomun）六四二年政變奪權，六六六年死後諸子內戰。唐羅聯盟（Tang–Silla alliance）於六六〇年滅百濟（Baekje）、六六八年滅高句麗。薩爾水一戰，雖使高句麗免於隋的征服，卻間接催生了更堅決的唐，最終加速了高句麗的滅亡。

要理解這一終結，須回溯朝鮮三國時期（Three Kingdoms period，約前五七—六六八年）的七百年角逐。半島三國的格局，並非簡單的三足鼎立，而是一個由地理、經濟與外部勢力共同塑造的動態體系。

高句麗（Goguryeo） 由朱蒙（Jumong）於前三十七年建國，據有朝鮮半島北部與滿洲（今中國東北）山地。其地山高谷深、冬寒夏短，天然利於防禦而不利於農耕，故其國以山城（mountain fortress）為骨、以騎兵為鋒，形成一種高度軍事化的社會結構。高句麗與中原王朝的關係貫穿其始終：它既接受過中原的冊封，也多次與中原兵戎相見；隋唐三征，正是這一長期對抗的高潮。高句麗之於中國帝國，是東北邊疆最頑固的對手；之於半島，則是最強大的霸主。

百濟（Baekje） 由溫祚（Onjo）於前十八年建國，據有半島西南的肥沃平原與漫長海岸。其地宜農、宜商、宜航，故百濟以海洋經濟立國，成為半島與日本之間的文化與貿易橋樑。百濟與日本大和朝廷關係極密，佛教、漢字、工匠、典籍經由百濟東傳，深刻塑造了日本早期國家。然而，富庶也意味著脆弱：百濟缺乏高句麗那樣的山地縱深，一旦海軍失利、盟友背棄，便無險可守。

新羅（Silla） 由朴赫居世（Park Hyeokgeose）於前五十七年建國，地處半島東南，山地隔絕、起步最弱。正因弱，新羅發展出一套獨特的生存策略：以花郎（Hwarang） 精英青年團凝聚尚武精神與政治忠誠，以外交縱橫取代正面硬拼。新羅先與高句麗、百濟周旋以自保，繼而與唐結盟以滅百濟、高句麗，最後翻臉驅逐唐軍——三步走完，半島遂歸一統。

三國的興亡，可循一條清晰的因果鏈：六六〇年，唐羅聯軍滅百濟，百濟遺族求救於日本，日本出兵，於六六三年在白村江（Battle of Baekgang）被唐羅聯軍擊潰，百濟復國無望；六六八年，唐羅聯軍滅高句麗，高句麗末期內亂（淵蓋蘇文 Yeon Gaesomun 六四二年政變奪權，六六六年死後諸子內戰）使其無力抵抗南北夾擊；六七〇至六七六年，羅唐戰爭（Silla–Tang War） 爆發，新羅擊退試圖在半島建立直接統治的唐軍，首次在大同江以南統一朝鮮。

高句麗的滅亡，尤其值得與薩爾水並觀。它並非敗於某一戰，而是敗於一種結構性困境：山地防禦使其能贏下每一次防守戰，卻無法把防守勝利轉化為戰略主動；連年戰爭耗盡了它的人口與經濟，而內部分裂又使其在唐朝的第二次、第三次打擊面前喪失了統一指揮。薩爾水是它最輝煌的勝利，也是它衰亡的起點——因為正是這場慘勝，使隋亡而唐興，使更堅決、更有耐心的對手出現在它的邊境上。

新羅統一之後，半島進入統一新羅時代（Unified Silla，六六八—九三五年），佛教興盛，文化繁榮，與唐的交流達到高峰。此後，高麗（Goryeo，九一八—一三九二年） 取代新羅，統一 anywhere半島，「Korea」之名即源於此；再後是朝鮮（Joseon，一三九二—一九一〇年），以儒家治國，定都漢陽（Hanyang，今首爾 Seoul）。從三國到統一新羅，從高麗到朝鮮，半島政治格局的數次重塑，無不與中原王朝的興衰、物流能力與軍事投射能力相互糾纏。薩爾水一戰，正是這一糾纏最劇烈的一次爆發。

結語

隋文帝於開皇年間重建度量衡、鑄造五銖，看似只是一場技術性的經濟整頓；他開廣通渠、疏山陽瀆，看似只是為長安運糧；其子煬帝續開通濟、永濟、江南諸渠，又三征高句麗，看似是擴張與虛榮的疊加。然而這些看似互不相干的事實，折射出同一條歷史原理：在一個疆域遼闊、經濟重心南移、軍事威脅在北的帝國裡，統一的政治權威必須同時是統一的標準制定者、統一的物流組織者和統一的軍事動員者；三者一體，缺一不可，而三者皆以民力為最終的成本承擔者。

隋文帝重建度量衡，是因為四百年分裂銷毀了標準的母本——沒有統一標準，南北無法在同一經濟體內循環；他開廣通渠、煬帝續開運河網絡，是因為首都與邊防無法就地自養，糧食必須從南方以規模化方式北運——沒有統一物流，標準統一了也無物可流；他三征高句麗，是因為北疆安全是帝國存續的前提，而遠征的後勤又必須依賴運河與道路——沒有統一的動員，物流與標準都失去意義。三者互為條件，構成一個閉環：標準使物流可計量，物流使動員可實現，動員又反過來要求標準與物流的持續供給。

但這個閉環的成本，最終落在農民身上。運河的河工、遠征的征夫、賦稅的輸納，無一不是民力。隋朝以三十七年之短，完成了標準、物流、動員三重統一，卻也在同一過程中耗盡了民力，於是閉環在煬帝手中斷裂，王朝隨之傾覆。後繼的唐朝繼承了這個閉環的基礎設施，卻以更緩的節奏、更輕的徭役加以維持，於是「隋朝開河，唐朝受益」。

由此可以總結出一條貫穿中國帝國史的原理：統一從來不是一次性的政治事件，而是一項需要持續以基礎設施供養的系統工程；標準、物流、動員三者構成這一系統的三根支柱，而民力是支撐三者的地基。地基一旦被壓垮，再宏偉的系統也會在瞬間崩塌。 隋朝的興亡，正是這條原理最慘烈也最清晰的註腳。

From Qin Standardization to the Grand Canal: How the Chinese Empire Used Infrastructure to Counter Geography and Division

From Standard Unification to Mobilization—The Logic of Imperial Rise and Fall from an Infrastructure Perspective

Introduction

The history of the Chinese empire is, at its foundation, a history of infrastructure. Qin Shi Huang unified weights and measures in 221 BC; the Han inherited and modified the Qin system; yet nearly four centuries of division after the fall of the Han in 220 AD destroyed all of it. Emperor Wen of Sui reunified China in 589 AD and had to rebuild weights, measures, and currency from scratch. What supported this reconstruction was an unprecedented canal network. The canals exhausted the Sui's manpower, yet left centuries of infrastructure dividends for the Tang, Jin, Yuan, Ming, and Qing, and ultimately elevated Beijing from a frontier garrison town to the imperial capital. This essay follows this causal chain, examining how the Chinese empire used canals and roads to counter geographic constraints and political division, and uses the story of the Korean Three Kingdoms and Goguryeo as a mirror of this logic within the East Asian international system.

Chapter 1: The Fragility of Qin's Unified Standards

In 221 BC, Qin Shi Huang completed the first great unification in Chinese history and immediately implemented "the same script for writing, the same track for carts, and unified weights and measures." This was not a simple administrative measure but a profound economic revolution. Before this, the foot, bushel, catty, and coin of each feudal state differed, and cross-regional trade required constant conversion. After unification, backed by central coercive power, Qin cast standard bronze weights and measures and issued the "Ban Liang coin," giving a vast economy spanning the Yellow and Yangtze Rivers a unified language of measurement for the first time. The *Records of the Grand Historian* notes that he "unified the laws and standards of weights and measures, made cart tracks uniform, and standardized writing" — a few words that capture a silent revolution.

Yet the fate of these standards was bound tightly to the fate of the Qin dynasty. Qin Shi Huang died in 210 BC, and the Qin collapsed in 207 BC, lasting only fifteen years. Qin standards depended on the central government's enforcement: county officials checked local weights and measures against "master copies" issued by the center, and private casting or private standards were severely punished. Once central authority collapsed, the enforcement chain broke. In the wars of the late Qin, official standard vessels were melted down or lost, and localities reverted to their own practices.

After the Han Dynasty (202 BC–220 AD) was established, a key phenomenon in Chinese institutional history emerged—"Han cheng Qin zhi" (the Han inherited the Qin system). Liu Bang, founder of the Han, did not start from scratch but wholesale inherited Qin's bureaucratic system, legal framework, and weights and measures. The early Han continued to use Qin's "Ban Liang coin," until Emperor Wu of Han (Han Wudi) carried out a major currency reform, abolishing the Ban Liang and issuing the "Wu Zhu coin." The Wu Zhu was regular in shape and standard in weight and circulated for about seven hundred years, becoming the longest-lasting coin in Chinese history. Emperor Wu also unified the state monopoly on salt and iron and implemented the "equal transport and price stabilization" system, pushing the empire's economic control to its peak.

Thus, during the four centuries of the two Han dynasties, China did possess a working set of unified standards. The problem was not that the Han lacked standards, but that Han standards were equally bound to the Han central government. When the Han fell in 220 AD, these standards lost their guarantor.

Chapter 2: Four Centuries of Division and the Destruction of Standards

After the fall of the Han, China entered its longest period of division. First came the Three Kingdoms (220–280 AD), with Wei, Shu, and Wu each casting their own coins and levying their own taxes. The Western Jin (265–316 AD) conquered Wu in 280 AD, briefly unifying China, but the unification lasted only about thirty years. The Jin royal family erupted in the "War of the Eight Princes" (291–306 AD), exhausting the state's strength; northern peoples rose up, Luoyang fell in 316 AD, and the Western Jin ended. The Jin court fled south and established the Eastern Jin (317–420 AD), controlling only the area south of the Yangtze; the north entered the "Sixteen Kingdoms" period, with regimes changing like a revolving lantern. After the Eastern Jin, the south saw the successive dynasties of Song, Qi, Liang, and Chen, while the north was unified by the Northern Wei, which later split into Eastern Wei, Western Wei, Northern Qi, and Northern Zhou. This is known as the Southern and Northern Dynasties (420–589 AD).

During these nearly four hundred years, the northern and southern economies evolved separately, and weights, measures, and currency diverged completely. A "catty" in the Northern Wei was roughly twice that of the Southern Dynasties; the "foot" differed in length between north and south, and the "bushel" differed in capacity. As for currency, each state cast coins frequently, with varying purity and weight; private casting prevailed, bad money drove out good, and the people gradually retreated to barter using silk and grain. The Northern Dynasties at one point abolished coins in favor of silk, while the Southern Dynasties repeatedly cast iron and lead coins, causing violent currency fluctuations. The *Book of Sui: Treatise on Food and Money* records that at the time "coins and goods were used interchangeably, and fraud proliferated" — a vivid portrait of this chaos.

One logic must be emphasized here: The Qin and Han standards did not fail by design; the state that guaranteed them ceased to exist. Standards are a public good, whose provision depends on the coercion and verification of central authority. Division meant multiple authorities coexisted, each adjusting standards to its own fiscal needs; over centuries, standards not only lost uniformity but even lost their "master copies"—no party possessed an original standard vessel to which they could return. When Emperor Wen of Sui reunified China in 589 AD, what he faced was not a set of "forgotten old standards" but an economic wasteland where "standards no longer existed."

Chapter 3: Emperor Wen of Sui—Rebuilding Standards from Zero and the Grand Canal Network

Emperor Wen of Sui (Sui Wendi, Yang Jian) founded the Sui in 581 AD and conquered Chen in 589 AD, ending nearly four centuries of division. The foremost economic problem he faced was precisely the total chaos of weights, measures, and currency.

His response was systematic. First, he unified weights and measures: promulgating standard rulers, bushels, and scales, requiring localities to manufacture according to the standard and verify periodically. Second, he rectified the currency: casting a new standard "Wu Zhu coin," strictly prohibiting private casting, and setting up checkpoints at passes to confiscate non-standard coins, making currency a trustworthy medium of exchange again. Third, he rectified household registration and taxation: implementing the "great search and appearance check" and "fixed tax quotas by household grade," uncovering hidden populations and rebuilding the state's fiscal foundation.

The edict of Emperor Wen quoted at the opening of this essay—"Since the Jin court moved south, the world has been in chaos, the four seas divided... carriage tracks differ, and the people are mired in misery"—is his own account of this historical wasteland. The phrase "carriage tracks differ" refers to the division of weights, measures, and institutions; "Heaven looked down and commanded Us to rule" is his political declaration of taking the rebuilding of unified standards as his mission. The *Book of Sui: Annals of Emperor Gaozu* also records that he "unified the realm and renewed all institutions," which corroborates this edict.

Beyond rebuilding standards, Emperor Wen's other great contribution was initiating the Grand Canal network. In 584 AD he first dug the Guangtong Canal, running from the Wei River near the capital Chang'an to the Yellow River, bypassing the shallow, sandy, and unnavigable stretches of the Wei. The reason for the canal was the capital's food problem: Chang'an sits on the Guanzhong Plain, surrounded by mountains and passes, easy to defend but difficult to attack; yet after more than a thousand years of intensive farming, the local land had deteriorated and could not feed the vast bureaucracy, army, and urban population of a unified empire. Grain had to come from the North China Plain—modern Henan, Hebei, and Shandong; but the water route from the Yellow River to the Wei was extremely difficult near Tongguan, and grain ships were often forced to unload and transport by ox cart for hundreds of miles, at high cost and great loss. The Guangtong Canal allowed grain ships to bypass the Wei's shallows and reach Chang'an directly—the "last mile" of feeding the capital of a unified empire. He later dredged the Shanyang Canal, connecting the Huai and Yangtze Rivers, laying the foundation for north-south through-transport. The canal network thus began.

Chapter 4: The Grand Canal Network—Scale, Purpose, and the Cost in Manpower

The Guangtong Canal was only the beginning. Emperor Wen's son, Emperor Yang of Sui (Yang Guang), expanded the canal into a nationwide network connecting five major river systems, over 2,700 kilometers in total.

  • The Tongji Canal (605 AD): running southeast from the eastern capital Luoyang, connecting to the Huai River and then the Yangtze, carrying grain and goods from the North China Plain directly to Luoyang.
  • The Shanyang Canal: dredged by Emperor Wen in 587 AD and expanded by Emperor Yang around 605 AD, connecting the Huai and Yangtze Rivers—a key north-south link.
  • The Yongji Canal (608 AD): running north from the Yellow River directly to Zhuojun (modern Beijing), built specifically to supply the campaigns against Goguryeo.
  • The Jiangnan Canal (610 AD): running south from the Yangtze to Hangzhou, tapping the wealth of the Yangtze Delta.

By 610 AD, the Sui had built a north-south artery connecting the Hai, Yellow, Huai, Yangtze, and Qiantang river systems. The network had three design purposes: first, to transport southern grain and wealth to the northern capital and frontier; second, to provide logistics for foreign wars; third, to stitch the newly unified state together economically, so that north and south were no longer two conquered halves but an internally circulating whole. This was entirely consistent with Emperor Wen's logic of unifying weights, measures, and currency—he was unifying not only units of measurement but the physical flow of goods.

Yet the cost of the canals was brutal, and it was one of the major factors in the Sui's downfall. First, the requisition of manpower. Emperor Yang conscripted millions of peasants; historical records state that one section of the Tongji Canal alone conscripted over a million laborers, and on some sections half the workers died from exhaustion, disease, and abuse, their bodies buried in the newly dug channels. The *Book of Sui: Treatise on Food and Money* records that "of the conscripted laborers, four or five in ten died"—the figure may be exaggerated, but the exhaustion of manpower is evident. Second, agricultural collapse. Young laborers were pulled from the fields for years; no one sowed or harvested, grain output plummeted, and famine spread. Third, tax oppression. The costs of canals, palaces, the Great Wall, and three expeditions against Goguryeo piled up; taxes and corvée exceeded what peasants could bear, and flight and tax resistance spread nationwide. Fourth, popular resentment and symbolic humiliation. Emperor Yang took lavish dragon-boat tours along the canals, with a fleet stretching a thousand *li*, and the counties along the route were forced to supply provisions; the people saw the canals as instruments of imperial vanity rather than public welfare.

Thus, canal construction, harsh taxes, and the Goguryeo expeditions together crushed the Sui. In 618 AD, Emperor Yang was killed by his subordinates in Yangzhou, and the Sui fell after thirty-seven years. The irony of history is that the succeeding Tang Dynasty (618–907 AD) reaped the benefits—"the Sui dug the canal, the Tang benefited"—and Tang prosperity rested to a considerable degree on the infrastructure the Sui had bought with manpower.

Chapter 5: The Road System—The Empire's Nervous System

If canals were the empire's arteries, roads were its nervous system. Canals solved the problem of "volume"—grain could not be moved at scale by cart; roads solved the problem of "speed"—armies and intelligence had to be measured in days and hours.

The Qin Straight Road (Qinzhi Dao): running directly from the capital region to the northern frontier, about 700–800 kilometers long, cutting through mountains and filling valleys, wide enough for multiple chariots abreast—called "the world's first highway." It was almost entirely for military purposes: when the Xiongnu attacked, Qin forces could march north rapidly along the straight road without detouring through valleys.

The Han Hexi Corridor route: linking outposts, garrisons, and beacon towers into a chain, projecting imperial power into Central Asia, establishing the Protectorate of the Western Regions, and bringing modern Xinjiang into China's sphere of influence for the first time.

The Tang postal relay (Yizhan) system: over 40,000 kilometers of roads, more than 1,600 relay stations, with horse changes, enabling urgent military messages to travel up to 250 kilometers per day. The efficiency of this system allowed the Tang court to respond quickly to frontier crises. The *Tang Administrative Code* records: "A relay station every thirty li; throughout the realm, one thousand six hundred and thirty-nine stations"—showing the precision of the system.

The Ming Nine Frontier Garrisons military roads: nine military defense zones spanning the northern frontier from west to east, with military roads connecting the garrisons, built specifically to transport artillery, grain, and troops.

The logic of combined water and land transport was now complete: canals carried grain, roads carried troops; canals were slow but massive, roads were fast but small in volume; the two complemented each other, forming the empire's logistical skeleton.

Chapter 6: Beijing—From Frontier Garrison to Imperial Capital

Before the Sui, Zhuojun/Beijing had never been the capital of a unified dynasty. It was always a frontier fortress: the Ji state of the Zhou, the Yan state of the Warring States, a Qin commandery, a Tang garrison town called Youzhou. It was an outpost against northern nomadic forces, not the center of an empire. This identity lasted for over a thousand years—Beijing was long a "city of guarding the frontier," not a "city of ruling the realm."

The turning point came with the Sui. The Sui set the northern terminus of the Yongji Canal at Zhuojun and built it into a military supply depot. This decision proved a revolutionary fact: with canal-borne grain, Beijing could support a vast population and army. The Goguryeo expeditions further strengthened Beijing's military positioning and subjected its logistical capacity to a stress test: although the war failed, Beijing's status as a northern military hub was fixed. The Sui's investment laid the first cornerstone for Beijing's future status as capital.

The Jin Dynasty (1115–1234 AD) established its capital at Zhongdu (modern Beijing) in 1153 AD. At its peak its territory covered roughly 2.3–2.5 million square kilometers, spanning North China, Manchuria, eastern Mongolia, and parts of the Russian Far East, and it ruled for over a century, proving that Beijing could function well as a capital. The Jin built the Jin Kou Canal and the Zha River Canal, connecting to the canal network so that grain could continuously enter the city. The Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368 AD) built Dadu (Khanbaliq) on the same site and straightened the canal, digging the Huitong and Tonghui canals; the Qing continued.

However, the Jin, Yuan, and Qing were all dynasties of ethnic minorities whose homelands lay in the north or northeast, and their choice of Beijing as capital had an internal logic of a "dual-facing capital"—ruling the Han lands to the south while remaining connected to their own people to the north. What truly deserves inquiry is: why would a Han Chinese dynasty also abandon its own homeland and move its capital from the south to the north? This is the story of the Ming Emperor Chengzu, Zhu Di, moving the capital from Nanjing to Beijing. It was not simply "inheriting the old institutions of the previous dynasty" but a strategic choice made with deep deliberation, enormous cost, and a profound impact on the political landscape of China for the next five hundred years.

1. Nanjing: The Starting Point of the Ming

Zhu Yuanzhang (Ming Taizu), founder of the Ming, proclaimed himself emperor in 1368 and established the capital at Yingtian (modern Nanjing). Nanjing had ample reasons to be the capital: it was Zhu Yuanzhang's base, the source of his regime's legitimacy; it was located in the south, close to the richest granaries in the country, with convenient water transport and abundant wealth; and it had the natural defense of the Yangtze. During the thirty-one years of the Hongwu reign, Nanjing functioned well as the capital, with palaces, government offices, and city walls built in succession and institutions gradually perfected.

But Nanjing had an inherent shortcoming as the capital of a unified empire: it was too far south. After the Ming was founded, the greatest military threat remained the Mongol forces that had retreated to the steppe (the Northern Yuan and later the Tatars and Oirats). Nanjing was thousands of miles from the northern border, and urgent frontier reports took over ten days to reach the throne, so the emperor's reaction to northern warfare was always one step slow. In his later years Zhu Yuanzhang recognized this problem and considered moving the capital, sending Crown Prince Zhu Biao to inspect Guanzhong and Luoyang to assess feasibility; but Zhu Biao died young and Zhu Yuanzhang was advanced in age, so the proposal was shelved.

2. Why Did Zhu Di Move the Capital to Beijing?

After the Jingnan Campaign, Zhu Di (Ming Chengzu), the Prince of Yan, usurped the throne in 1402 and still based his capital at Nanjing. But the idea of moving the capital had germinated from the day he ascended. Its motives can be grouped into four layers:

First, Beijing was his homeland and power base. Zhu Di had garrisoned Beiping (Beijing) as Prince of Yan for over twenty years, and his military staff, trusted generals, and local power base were all in the north. Nanjing was the world of the Jianwen Emperor's old officials, and Zhu Di always felt like a "guest" there. Moving the capital to Beijing meant relocating his base to the center of power and resolving his legitimacy anxiety once and for all.

Second, the practical needs of military defense. The Ming's greatest threat was on the northern steppe. Zhu Di was himself an emperor on horseback who personally led five campaigns against the Mongols and well understood the principle of "the Son of Heaven guards the national gate." He once stated bluntly in an edict: "Beiping is Our old fief, with palaces and princely residences, and its terrain is sufficient to control the northern desert." Establishing the capital at the front line was both a deterrent to frontier generals and a rallying of military morale.

Third, the economic and transport conditions had matured. Zhu Di did not ignore the food problem in moving the capital. He knew Beijing could not be self-sufficient, so he vigorously dredged the Grand Canal—in the ninth year of Yongle (1411 AD) he ordered Song Li, Chen Xuan, and others to reopen the Huitong Canal and dredge the Tonghui Canal, so that Jiangnan grain could reach Beijing directly. The canal's unimpeded flow was the precondition for the move. This is a continuation of the same logic by which the Sui used canals to feed a northern capital: without the canal, Beijing could not feed a capital; with the canal, Beijing could become one.

Fourth, political symbolism. Zhu Di had usurped the throne under the name of "Jingnan" and needed a grand political project to rebuild legitimacy. Building Beijing and moving the capital north was both a declaration of "the Son of Heaven guards the national gate" and a symbol of "inheriting the former dynasties' old capital and ruling the Hua-Yi order." From the fourth year of Yongle (1406 AD), the Beijing palace city (the later Forbidden City) began construction; it was completed in the eighteenth year of Yongle (1420 AD), and the capital was formally moved the following year.

3. The Costs and Controversies of the Move

The move to Beijing was not without opposition. During the Yongle reign many ministers objected, arguing that Nanjing was rich in revenue and convenient for water transport, while Beijing was remote in the northern borderlands and difficult to supply. Zhu Di suppressed dissent with a firm hand, but the actual costs were indeed enormous: building the Forbidden City and Beijing consumed countless manpower and resources; the grain transport system had to be rebuilt entirely, with millions of *shi* of grain shipped north from Jiangnan each year, becoming a heavy burden on Ming finances; and the land around Beijing was limited, so if the canal were blocked the capital would face the threat of running out of food.

Yet from a long-term perspective, the benefits of moving the capital far outweighed the costs. For more than two hundred years after Ming Chengzu, Beijing as the capital effectively integrated northern military defense with southern economic supply: the emperor sat in the north, directly controlling the frontier; the canal carried southern grain north continuously, feeding the capital and the frontier armies. "Using northern troops to control southern wealth"—this arrangement was finally fixed in the Ming and wholly inherited by the Qing.

4. A Complete Historical Thread

At this point, a complete thread of capital migration from the Sui to the Qing can be strung together:

  • Sui: built the Yongji Canal to Zhuojun, turning Beijing into a military supply depot, proving canals could feed the north.
  • Jin: established the capital at Zhongdu, built the Jin Kou and Zha River canals, making Beijing a capital for the first time and proving it could function.
  • Yuan: built Dadu, straightened the canal, establishing Beijing as the capital of a unified empire.
  • Ming: Chengzu, on the grounds that "the Son of Heaven guards the national gate," moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing, making it the capital of a Han dynasty as well, with the reopening of the canal as its precondition.
  • Qing: inherited the Ming capital arrangement and continued to supply Beijing via the canal.

Every step of this thread depends on one premise: the canal must remain open. The Sui dug the "pipeline," the Jin connected the "faucet," the Yuan straightened the pipeline, the Ming reopened silted sections and confirmed the arrangement by moving the capital, and the Qing maintained it. Beijing became the imperial capital for five hundred years not because it was optimal in any single dimension, but because it combined "the highest score in military defense" with "the sustainability of canal logistics."

Chapter 7: Why Establish the Capital in the North?

Before discussing Beijing, one must first see a larger pattern: before the Sui and Tang, the capitals of unified dynasties were almost all in the north. Qin's capital was Xianyang, Western Han's was Chang'an, Eastern Han's was Luoyang, Western Jin's was still Luoyang, and the Sui and Tang had two capitals, east and west, still mainly Chang'an and Luoyang. The Northern Song capital was Kaifeng, slightly southeast but still on the Yellow River line. It was the Jin, Yuan, Ming, and Qing that truly stabilized the capital farther north at Beijing.

This pattern was no accident. Economically alone, the south far surpassed the north: the lower Yangtze had been the nation's granary and fiscal center since the Tang, hence the saying "when Su and Hu are ripe, the realm has enough." In transport alone, the south was dense with waterways, convenient for grain transport, and Hangzhou and Yangzhou were the realm's great thoroughfares. Yet successive dynasties preferred to ship southern grain a thousand miles north by canal rather than move the capital south. Why? Because the choice of capital was never an economic question but a question of survival. In the empire's hierarchy of priorities, military defense and political legitimacy ranked above economic convenience; economic inconvenience could be remedied with infrastructure, but a gap in defense could end a dynasty overnight.

1. The Northern Steppe Was the Greatest Threat

This is the most fundamental and least compromiseable point. Since Qin Shi Huang built the Great Wall, the main military threat to the Chinese empire had always come from the northern steppe: the Xiongnu in the Qin-Han period, the Turks in the Sui-Tang period, the Mongols and Jurchens in the Song-Ming period—all pressed from the north. Nomadic cavalry was extremely mobile, covering hundreds of miles in a day and night; if the capital were in the south, by the time urgent frontier reports reached the throne, the enemy would already have breached the Great Wall and penetrated deep into the interior.

Therefore, successive emperors made the capital a forward operating base: the closer the emperor was to the army, the shorter the decision chain and the faster the response. Qin's Xianyang and Han's Chang'an were both in Guanzhong, overlooking the north from high ground; Ming Chengzu's move to Beijing was justified directly by "the Son of Heaven guards the national gate"—the emperor personally stationed at the gate was both a deterrent to frontier generals and a rallying of morale. By contrast, the Southern Song retreated to Hangzhou, far from the front; despite the natural barrier of the Yangtze, they could only defend passively and were ultimately swept away by the Mongols from north to south. Where the capital is, there the strategic center of gravity is; where the strategic center of gravity is, there the empire's lifeline is.

2. Political Legitimacy: The Mandate of Heaven Cannot Be Lightly Abandoned

The second point is political and cultural. The traditional core of Chinese civilization lay in the Yellow River basin—Guanzhong and the Central Plains were the dragon-rising lands of Zhou, Qin, Han, and Tang, the gathering place of ritual institutions, canonical texts, and great clans. To rule from the historical center was to conform to the narrative of the "Mandate of Heaven": the emperor sacrificed to Heaven and ancestors at Chang'an and Luoyang, continuing the orthodoxy of the Three Dynasties; to move south was to admit to being a mere regional regime and to lose the qualification of "common sovereign of the realm."

This was repeatedly borne out in history. In 1127, during the Jingkang Incident, the Song court crossed south and established its capital at Hangzhou. Although the Southern Song was economically prosperous and culturally brilliant, historians always regarded it as a "rump regime cowering in a corner"; the call for northern expeditions never ceased throughout the Southern Song, precisely because "recovering the Central Plains" was the source of its legitimacy. Further back, after the Eastern Jin crossed south, the "émigré-versus-native" conflict between northern gentry and southern natives lasted a hundred years, rooted in the same fact: the southern regime never dared to admit it had abandoned the north, for once it admitted so, the Mandate of Heaven would no longer belong to it.

3. The Formula for Choosing a Capital

Abstracting the above factors, one can write a rough formula:

Capital = (Military Defense + Political Legitimacy + Threat Management) − (Logistics Cost + Local Opposition)

Plugging in successive capitals, the results are clear:

  • Hangzhou, Yangzhou: extremely low logistics cost (located in the granary and at canal hubs), little local opposition; but military defense is nearly zero (once the Yangtze falls there is no natural barrier), and political legitimacy for a unified empire is insufficient. Hence they are suitable as economic metropolises, not as imperial capitals.
  • Chang'an, Luoyang: high military defense and political legitimacy, but extremely high logistics cost (Guanzhong's land had deteriorated, requiring dependence on grain transport), and Guanzhong's local forces were deeply entrenched. Hence the Sui and Tang had to mobilize the whole country to build canals and dredge transport routes to compensate for logistics.
  • Beijing: highest military defense (facing the steppe directly), most direct threat management (the emperor at the front line), political legitimacy given new meaning by minority dynasties in the Yuan, Ming, and Qing; logistics cost equally high, compensated by the canal network continuously expanded since the Sui and Tang.

What the emperors calculated was exactly this: better to bear the "logistics tax" than the existential risk of "no defense." Canals could be built and grain moved; however high the cost, it was calculable. But once the capital fell, the dynasty would immediately collapse—and that was incalculable.

4. Controlling the Aristocracy and Local Forces

There is another factor, often overlooked but extremely realistic: the location of the capital was also a product of the emperor's strategic contest with local forces.

The south grew wealthier after the Tang, and Jiangnan gentry, merchant guilds, and salt merchants accumulated enormous economic power. If the capital were in the south, the emperor would be surrounded by these local forces and easily held hostage or marginalized. Setting the capital in the north kept the court at some distance from southern wealth, making it easier both to draw southern wealth through grain transport and to use northern military groups to balance southern economic groups. The Yuan, Ming, and Qing all established their capitals at Beijing while continuously shipping southern grain north via the canal—precisely the embodiment of this "northern troops controlling southern wealth" arrangement.

Conversely, southern wealth was not entirely without a voice. The Southern Song's choice of Hangzhou was to some extent the result of a compromise between southern economic groups and the refugee court: the court needed southern money and grain, and the south needed the court's protection, and the two reached a balance at Hangzhou. But this balance was fragile—once a strong enemy pressed from the north, a southern capital lacking strategic depth would bear the brunt.

5. Summary: Beijing Is the Endpoint of This Logic

Taking the four points together, Beijing ultimately became the imperial capital not because it was optimal in any single dimension, but because it scored highest in military defense and threat management, and its logistical shortfall could be compensated precisely by the canal network continuously built since the Sui and Tang.

The Jin, Yuan, and Qing were minority dynasties whose homelands were all in the north or northeast; establishing the capital at Beijing allowed them both to rule the Han lands to the south and to remain connected to their own people to the north—the typical "dual-facing capital." Ming Chengzu rose as the Prince of Yan, and Beijing was his base; moving the capital there was both a political and a military necessity. Thus, a capital trajectory that ran from Qin's Xianyang, Han's Chang'an, Sui-Tang's Chang'an and Luoyang, and Northern Song's Kaifeng, shifting east and north, finally settled at Beijing in the Yuan, Ming, and Qing.

This settlement was not an endpoint but a temporary balance in a long game: southern grain and wealth, northern armies and frontier defense, the canal as the umbilical cord connecting the two, and the emperor seated at the northern end of the cord, holding a sword in one hand and grain in the other. Where the capital is has never been a geographic question, but a question of how the empire allocates its resources for survival.

Chapter 8: The Battle of Salsu and the History of the Korean Three Kingdoms

The Battle of Salsu became one of the important causes of the Sui's fall. Emperor Yang's three campaigns against Goguryeo mobilized manpower and resources beyond what the empire could bear: canal construction, military transport, and millions of conscripts, all combined, caused "farming to miss its season and fields to lie fallow" (*Book of Sui: Treatise on Food and Money*). And the victorious side, Goguryeo, was also severely weakened by years of resisting the Sui: the war exhausted its treasury, manpower, and agriculture; though its mountain fortresses won, it had no strength to recover. This is precisely the meaning of a Pyrrhic victory—winning the battle but losing the vitality.

After the Sui fell, the Tang took up this unfinished task. Emperor Taizong of Tang (Li Shimin) personally campaigned against Goguryeo in 645 AD; though he failed to take Ansi City, he further exhausted Goguryeo. Internal turmoil followed: Yeon Gaesomun seized power in a coup in 642 AD, and after his death in 666 AD his sons fought a civil war. The Tang–Silla alliance destroyed Baekje in 660 AD and Goguryeo in 668 AD. The Battle of Salsu, though it saved Goguryeo from Sui conquest, indirectly gave rise to a more determined Tang and ultimately accelerated Goguryeo's destruction.

To understand this end, one must go back to the seven-hundred-year contest of the Korean Three Kingdoms period (roughly 57 BC–668 AD). The three-kingdom configuration on the peninsula was not a simple three-way standoff but a dynamic system shaped jointly by geography, economy, and external forces.

Goguryeo, founded by Jumong in 37 BC, occupied the mountains of the northern peninsula and Manchuria (modern Northeast China). Its terrain—high mountains, deep valleys, cold winters and short summers—naturally favored defense over farming, so the state was built on mountain fortresses as its bones and cavalry as its spearhead, forming a highly militarized social structure. Goguryeo's relations with the Central Plains dynasties ran through its entire history: it both accepted Central Plains investiture and repeatedly came to blows with the Central Plains; the Sui and Tang campaigns were the climax of this long confrontation. To the Chinese empire, Goguryeo was the most stubborn adversary on the northeastern frontier; to the peninsula, it was the strongest hegemon.

Baekje, founded by Onjo in 18 BC, occupied the fertile plains and long coastline of the southwestern peninsula. Its land was suited to farming, commerce, and navigation, so Baekje built a maritime economy and became the cultural and commercial bridge between the peninsula and Japan. Baekje had extremely close ties with the Japanese Yamato court; Buddhism, Chinese characters, artisans, and texts passed east through Baekje, profoundly shaping the early Japanese state. Yet wealth also meant vulnerability: Baekje lacked the mountain depth of Goguryeo, and once its navy failed and its allies deserted, it had no natural defense.

Silla, founded by Park Hyeokgeose in 57 BC, was located in the southeastern peninsula, isolated by mountains and the weakest at the start. Precisely because it was weak, Silla developed a unique survival strategy: it used the Hwarang elite youth corps to forge martial spirit and political loyalty, and replaced direct confrontation with diplomatic maneuvering. Silla first maneuvered among Goguryeo and Baekje to protect itself, then allied with Tang to destroy Baekje and Goguryeo, and finally turned on Tang and expelled its armies—three steps, and the peninsula was unified.

The rise and fall of the three kingdoms follows a clear causal chain: in 660 AD the Tang–Silla alliance destroyed Baekje; Baekje survivors appealed to Japan, Japan sent troops, and in 663 AD at the Battle of Baekgang the Japanese were crushed by the Tang–Silla forces, ending Baekje's hopes of restoration; in 668 AD the Tang–Silla alliance destroyed Goguryeo, whose late internal turmoil (Yeon Gaesomun's coup in 642 AD and his sons' civil war after his death in 666 AD) left it unable to resist a north-south pincer; from 670 to 676 AD the Silla–Tang War broke out, and Silla drove back the Tang forces attempting to establish direct rule on the peninsula, unifying Korea south of the Taedong River for the first time.

Goguryeo's destruction deserves to be viewed alongside Salsu. It was not defeated in any single battle but by a structural dilemma: mountain defense allowed it to win every defensive battle but could not convert defensive victories into strategic initiative; years of war exhausted its population and economy, and internal division left it without unified command against the Tang's second and third strikes. Salsu was its most brilliant victory and also the starting point of its decline—because it was precisely this Pyrrhic victory that caused the Sui to fall and the Tang to rise, bringing a more determined and patient adversary to its borders.

After Silla's unification, the peninsula entered the Unified Silla period (668–935 AD), with Buddhism flourishing, culture prospering, and exchanges with Tang reaching a peak. Thereafter, Goryeo (918–1392 AD) replaced Silla and unified the peninsula—the name "Korea" derives from it; and then Joseon (1392–1910 AD), which ruled by Confucianism and established its capital at Hanyang (modern Seoul). From the Three Kingdoms to Unified Silla, from Goryeo to Joseon, the several remakings of the peninsula's political landscape were all intertwined with the rise and fall of Central Plains dynasties, their logistical capacity, and their military projection capability. The Battle of Salsu was the most intense eruption of this entanglement.

Conclusion

Emperor Wen of Sui's rebuilding of weights and measures and casting of the Wu Zhu coin during the Kaihuang era may seem merely a technical economic rectification; his digging of the Guangtong Canal and dredging of the Shanyang Canal may seem merely about transporting grain to Chang'an; his son Emperor Yang's continuation of the Tongji, Yongji, and Jiangnan canals, and his three campaigns against Goguryeo, may seem an accumulation of expansion and vanity. Yet these seemingly unrelated facts reflect the same historical principle: In an empire with vast territory, an economic center shifting south, and military threats in the north, a unified political authority must simultaneously be a unified standard-setter, a unified logistics organizer, and a unified military mobilizer; the three are one body, none can be missing, and all three ultimately rest on manpower as the final cost-bearer.

Emperor Wen rebuilt weights and measures because four centuries of division had destroyed the master copies of standards—without unified standards, north and south could not circulate within the same economy; he dug the Guangtong Canal and Emperor Yang continued the canal network because the capital and the frontier could not feed themselves locally, and grain had to be shipped north from the south at scale—without unified logistics, unified standards would have nothing to move; he campaigned three times against Goguryeo because northern frontier security was the precondition for the empire's survival, and the logistics of the expeditions had to rely on canals and roads—without unified mobilization, both logistics and standards would lose meaning. The three were mutually conditioning, forming a closed loop: standards made logistics measurable, logistics made mobilization achievable, and mobilization in turn demanded the continued supply of standards and logistics.

But the cost of this closed loop ultimately fell on the peasants. The canal laborers, the conscripts of the expeditions, the taxpayers—all were manpower. The Sui, in only thirty-seven years, completed the triple unification of standards, logistics, and mobilization, but in the same process exhausted its manpower, so the loop broke in Emperor Yang's hands and the dynasty collapsed with it. The succeeding Tang inherited the infrastructure of this loop but maintained it at a slower pace and with lighter corvée, hence "the Sui dug the canal, the Tang benefited."

From this one can summarize a principle running through Chinese imperial history: Unification is never a one-time political event but a systemic project that must be continuously sustained by infrastructure; standards, logistics, and mobilization form the three pillars of this system, and manpower is the foundation supporting all three. Once the foundation is crushed, the grandest system will collapse in an instant. The rise and fall of the Sui is the most tragic and clearest footnote to this principle.
